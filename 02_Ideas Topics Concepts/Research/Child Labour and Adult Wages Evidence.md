# Child Labour and Adult Wages Evidence

> [!success] NBER paper verified 2026-11-09
> I opened and read the NBER paper (abstract, introduction and model). Authors: **Prashant Bharadwaj, Leah K. Lakdawala and Nicholas Li**, October 2013, confirmed. The other sources (ScienceDirect, the Firozabad case studies, the state-level study) are still unverified.

> [!important] Finding that changes the picture
> The paper's model **assumes** employers treat child and adult labor as substitutes, and footnote 9 cites evidence for this. Substitution is the first step of the user's mechanism. The paper also found that after the ban, poor households' **food spending and calorie intake fell**. That's a real, documented link between child-labor dynamics and hunger, but it runs through the ban pushing down child wages, not through children displacing adults.

## Summary
This note tests the user's proposed mechanism for [[GSH-005 Shell-Company Data Center Case]]: that child labor pushes down adults' wages and jobs, so families can afford less food. **Partly supported:** the NBER paper says employers treat child and adult labor as substitutes, and it links child-labor economics to lower calorie intake, though by a different route. No study I found directly shows the full chain from displaced adults to hunger.

## Key facts (with page/section)
- ✓ After India's 1986 child-labor ban, **child wages fell and child labor increased**, mostly in poorer families. The relative fall in wages happened in manufacturing, the sector the ban targeted. School enrollment fell. (NBER w19602) The "about 4%" figure came from a search summary; I didn't find it in the part of the paper I read.
- ✓ Households were worse off after the ban: total spending, food spending, non-staple share of calories and asset holdings all fell significantly, though the effects were small.
- ✓ The model assumes "child and adult labor are substitutes" (footnote 12). Footnote 9 cites Doran (2013) as evidence that employers treat them that way.
- ✓ Edmonds (2007), as quoted in the paper, found no study of restrictions on work "that would meet current standards of evidence."
- Higher minimum wages for adults reduced child work in domestic chores and household businesses, and adult employment rose in rural areas. (ScienceDirect, *Child labor and the minimum wage: Evidence from India*)
- Older case studies report children paid about 60% of the adult wage for the same work, for example in Firozabad's glass-bangle industry. (Small, dated studies)
- A 2007 review by Edmonds reportedly found no study of work restrictions that met current standards of evidence.
- A state-level study reportedly found that child and adult labor rates rose together, not against each other.

## Useful quotes
From Bharadwaj, Lakdawala & Li, NBER w19602, Oct 2013 (read directly):
> "we show that child wages decrease and child labor increases after the ban"

> "families use child labor to reach subsistence constraints and where child wages decrease in response to bans, leading poor families to utilize more child labor"

> "along various margins of household expenditure, consumption, calorie intake and asset holdings, households are worse off after the ban"

> "Existing empirical evidence suggests that employers treat child and adult labor as substitutes. See for example Doran (2013)." (footnote 9)

## My interpretation
- **Partly supported.** Employers do appear to treat children and adults as substitutes, which is the first step of the user's mechanism. Doran (2013) is the lead to follow for whether child labor actually displaces adults.
- The full chain (displaced adults, then lower family income, then hunger) still isn't directly shown. But the link between child-labor economics, subsistence and calorie intake is now documented, in the paper's own setting of a ban.
