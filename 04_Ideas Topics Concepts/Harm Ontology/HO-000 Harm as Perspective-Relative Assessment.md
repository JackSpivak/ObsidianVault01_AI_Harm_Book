# HO-000 — Harm as Perspective-Relative Assessment

Index: [[_Harm Ontology Bookmark Index]] · Root

## Why this is distinct
- Most harm ontologies, including v1/v2 of the Compendium, assert *what harm is*. This one models harm as something **judged**: neutral things happen, and assessments made from perspectives decide whether they count as harm.

## Anticipated direction
- A layered model: Factor → CausalRole → Effect, judged by Assessments under Evaluative Standards and taken from Perspectives. Divergence between assessments is first-class, and a LegalResponse layer is to follow. It will eventually be expressed in RDF/OWL, with SHACL profiles, reusing expert vocabularies.

## Conceptual territory
- It revisits the territory of [[Interdisciplinary_Harm_Ontology_Compendium v2]] from a different angle. v1 filed rival theories (experiential, interest-based, rights-based, flourishing) as sibling *kinds* of harm. Here they become standards that are applied to things.

## Summary
- Theories of harm describe and judge harm; they don't classify it. One event can be harmful under one theory and harmless under another, and the model has to hold both verdicts without contradiction. Every element below this root follows from that commitment.

## Original wording
> "In this sense, Accounts do not actually classify (or subclassify) harms. I think they describe harm.  Specifically they describe a theory within which a harm might be acknowledged or recognized or literally considered to be a harm.  thus a single \"harm\" may or may not be harmful from different philosophical perspectives. this isnt a problem unless one tries to subclass a \"thing' called a \"harm\" under a thing called an Account."

## Real-world examples
- Covert AI profiling of a user who never notices: no harm under the experiential view, harm under the rights-based view, and harm over time under the capacity view.

## Why it matters to the book
- Many AI harms are invisible under the experiential test and obvious under the rights or capacity tests. A model that shows that disagreement explains why such harms are easy to dismiss.
