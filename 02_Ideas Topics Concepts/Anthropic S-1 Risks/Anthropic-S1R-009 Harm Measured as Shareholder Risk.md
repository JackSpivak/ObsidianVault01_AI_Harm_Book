# Harm Measured as Shareholder Risk

## The idea - Summary
- In an IPO filing, every harm to people (injury, lost privacy, damage to mental health, even existential risk) ends in the same phrase: it "could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations." The disclosure is legally binding, which makes it credible, but it measures harm by its cost to investors. The same section treats safety precautions as a competitive risk, and treats falling behind as a "self-reinforcing or compounding cycle," which explains the pressure to accelerate.

## The idea - Original wording
- > "Harm Measured as Shareholder Risk — The disclosure framing in which every harm ends as "adversely affect our business""
  - **Attribution:** Claude: Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5)
- > "(interpretation) Risk-factor sections are written to limit legal liability: disclose everything so nobody can later sue over an omission. That makes the admissions credible, because it is an offense to misstate them. It also shapes their framing, because each harm ends in "adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.""
  - **Attribution:** Claude: Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5), in [[Anthropic S-1 Risk Factors 2026]]
- > "pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity, particularly if such systems were misused, replicated, weaponized, or allowed to operate with a high degree of autonomy. We have observed that our models are contributing to the pace of our own research, and these risks will increase as AI systems substantially automate AI research and development if, for example, they accelerate AI progress beyond our ability to evaluate and safeguard new models, which could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations." (p. 32)

## Real-world examples
- The whole Risk Factors section. The p. 32 sentence that goes from "existential risks to humanity" to "results of operations" is the sharpest single example.

## Why it matters to the book
- It could be a framing device: the gap between what a harm means to the person who suffers it and how it is written down in the company's accounts.
