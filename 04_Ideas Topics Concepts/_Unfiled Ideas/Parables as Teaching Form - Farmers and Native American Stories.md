# Parables as Teaching Form - Farmers and Native American Stories

## Why this is distinct
- It asks how pervasive parables should be in the book, using the Chinese farmer story and Native American stories as examples. It is a question of form (how the book teaches), not of content.

## Anticipated direction
- Decide where parables appear, how many, and whether they are framed as teaching devices or left open.
- Use parables as test cases for the ontology, which is already done for the Chinese farmer in [[HO-002 Ontology Stress-Test Suite]].

## Conceptual territory
- Connects to the Chinese farmer parable in [[HO-002 Ontology Stress-Test Suite]] and to the Mencius and classical sources in [[Test Case Corpus for Harm Detection]].

## Summary
- Parables teach through situations rather than rules. They leave the moral open for the listener.
- The book should ask whether parables fit the questions-over-answers method ([[Questions Over Answers and the Book as Prompt]]).
- Native American and other oral traditions need consulting councils, not just quotation (see [[Council Roster Expansion]]).

## Real-world examples
- The Chinese "lost horse" (Sai Weng) story, already used as a horizon test in [[HO-002 Ontology Stress-Test Suite]].

## Why it matters to the book
- Parables give the book a form that readers already know how to read, which helps with accessibility and with the questions-first method.

## Open questions
- How many parables can the book carry before it becomes a collection?
- Who may tell a traditional story, and under what framing?
