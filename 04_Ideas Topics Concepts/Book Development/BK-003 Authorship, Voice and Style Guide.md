# Authorship, Voice and Style Guide

## Why this is distinct
- It asks who writes the book, from what perspective and in what style, for what age audience. It proposes building a style guide from successful books and taking voice models from the people who shaped culture. No existing card sets a voice or audience decision.

## Anticipated direction
- Decide the narrator's perspective and the readers' age range, then test both.
- Analyse New York Times bestsellers for structural and stylistic patterns, and write a style guide from the results.
- Study voices that changed ideas (speeches, pamphlets, trial arguments, founding documents) and what made them persuasive.
- Set up an AI council of writers and editors, and a council of trial advocates and framers, as sources of voice tests.

## Conceptual territory
- Extends the "Council of Writers and Editors" item in [[structured-inquiry-book-notes]] and the editorial mechanics in [[BK-011 Council Dialogue Mechanics - Question-Led Councils]].

## Summary
- Who writes this book, and from which perspective? Is the narrator the author, the AI, a sage, or a chorus?
- Audience: which age group, and what reading level? Plain language is the default, to keep the book accessible.
- Style: the author asks whose voice and persuasive power created the most culture-changing ideas, and how those voices should inform this book.
- A style guide built from bestsellers can make the voice concrete and testable.

## Real-world examples
- Plain-language nonfiction that makes hard subjects accessible (to be selected and checked).
- Founding documents whose clarity and persuasive structure still shape public argument.

## Why it matters to the book
- Voice decides whether readers trust the book's claims. A voice that is wrong for its readers will lose them before the argument starts.

## Open questions
- Is the narrator the author, an AI, or a shifting voice?
- What is the target age, and does the book need an edition for each?
- Which bestsellers are the right baseline?
