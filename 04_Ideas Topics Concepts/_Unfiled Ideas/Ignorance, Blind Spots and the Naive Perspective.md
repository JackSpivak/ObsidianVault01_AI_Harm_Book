# Ignorance, Blind Spots and the Naive Perspective

## Why this is distinct
- It treats ignorance as a perspective in its own right. It adds a deliberate naive or childlike viewpoint, and the "wise fool" figure, as sources of questions that expert councils miss.

## Anticipated direction
- Build a blind-spot register: what the assessment knows, what it knows it does not know, and what it does not know it does not know.
- Add a naive-perspective council voice (childlike or wise-fool) whose job is to ask the question nobody in the room thinks to ask.

## Conceptual territory
- Relates to the council of children and innocence in [[Council Roster Expansion]].
- Pairs with [[Assessor Situation and Standpoint]], since blind spots depend on position.

## Summary
- The author asks: what do I know that I don't know, and what do I not know that I don't know?
- The sophomore, the wise fool and the child are figures who ask naive questions and sometimes see what the expert cannot.
- Blind spots should be recorded as part of each assessment, so the ontology can say what it cannot see.

## Real-world examples
- Children asking why a rule exists, exposing an unexamined assumption.
- Post-mortems that found the risk everyone had silently assumed away.

## Why it matters to the book
- The book's blind spots are the places where the AI's ontology can quietly drift. A naive-perspective council is a check on that drift (see [[The Self-Revising Harm Ontology]]).

## Open questions
- How is a blind-spot register kept without becoming a catalogue of everything?
- Can a naive perspective be modelled without pretending to be naive?
- Which blind spots are the most dangerous for AI assessments?
