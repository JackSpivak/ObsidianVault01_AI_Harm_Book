# Urgency, Priorities and Who Cares

## Why this is distinct
- It asks how competing needs, interests and beliefs set the agenda by defining urgency and priorities, and who cares about the harm and who does not. It links care to the inquiry's aims, and it brings in a quote from Robert Percy and the Buddhist aim to minimise suffering.

## Anticipated direction
- Map stakeholders by what they care about, why, and what they would give up for it.
- Record who does not care, and why. Indifference is data.
- Test whether the book's urgency matches the stakeholders' urgency.

## Conceptual territory
- Relates to [[Assessor Situation and Standpoint]] (what position each party holds), [[Harm-Opportunity Tension and Weighing]] (how interests are weighed) and [[Stakes of the Inquiry]].

## Summary
- Urgency and priority are set by whose interests are most pressing, which depends on standpoint.
- Care should be treated as a variable: who cares, why, and what care has to do with the inquiry.
- The Buddhist aim of minimising suffering is one such standard of urgency. It sets a priority different from one based on profit or control.

## Real-world examples
- Public-health priorities that rank diseases by burden and by political attention, and differ.
- Corporate risk registers that rank harms by financial exposure rather than by suffering.

## Why it matters to the book
- The book's own urgency depends on who it persuades. Showing who cares, and why, makes the agenda explicit.

## Open questions
- Is care a property of the assessor or a standard the assessment must meet?
- How does the minimising-suffering standard compare with the weighing rule?
- Which quote is the Percy one, and is it accurately attributed?
