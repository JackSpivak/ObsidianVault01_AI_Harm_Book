# Council Dialogue Mechanics - Question-Led Councils

## Why this is distinct
- It sets out how councils should work, not who sits on them. Councils ask questions rather than giving verdicts, engage with the idea that interests each member most rather than with the previous speaker, and edit through friction and synthesis.

## Anticipated direction
- Each council, for each type of harm, weighs in by asking questions.
- In writing and editing, members propose concrete edits, comment on each other's edits, and either hold a tension, agree on intent, or synthesize two or more responses.

## Conceptual territory
- Extends the "Council Engagement & Harm Assessment Rules" and "Iterative Editing Dynamics" sections of [[structured-inquiry-book-notes]].
- Depends on [[BK-010 Council Roster Expansion]] for who sits on each council.

## Summary
- Harm inquiry: each council asks questions about each type of harm from its own domain. The questions, not the answers, guide the inquiry.
- Non-linear engagement: members respond to the idea that interests them, not to whoever spoke last. This prevents the discussion from following the loudest thread.
- Iterative editing: members propose rewording, changes of style or structure, simplification and clarification. Others respond to those proposals. Tension is kept where it is productive, consensus is reached where intent is shared, and a higher perspective is used to synthesize two positions.
- The editorial test is whether a passage feels uncanny to the councils that know the relevant part of human experience.

## Original wording
> "For the various types of harm, perhaps each individual council could weigh in with questions ... For writing and editing see if the council could engage with the idea that interests individual members the most rather than the previous speaker. Editing benefits from iteration and alternate perspectives. Individual council members could propose edits - rewording, changes in style or structure, simplification, clarifications - and individual members could comment on those responses and differ developing a tension, or agreeing focusing intent, or elevating perspective to to synthesize or bring two other responses together"

## Real-world examples
- Editorial rooms where writers, editors and fact-checkers each mark up the same draft.
- Multi-stakeholder review boards that run on written questions before any decision.

## Why it matters to the book
- The book's method depends on councils that disagree usefully. Without these rules, councils would collapse into a series of agreeing summaries.

## Open questions
- How are tensions recorded so that a later reader can see what was left open?
- What stops a synthesis from erasing the tension it was meant to resolve?
- Can an AI member ask a question that a human member could not?
