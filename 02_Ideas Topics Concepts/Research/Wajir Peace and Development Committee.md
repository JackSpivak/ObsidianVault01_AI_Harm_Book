# Wajir Peace and Development Committee

> [!warning] Verification status
> The core facts and outcome quotes were checked against the Better Evidence Project page above (fetched 2026-10-29). The 29 September 1993 declaration date and the origin story credited to Dekha Ibrahim come from search summaries of the other sources and have **not** been checked directly.

## Summary
- Clan violence in Wajir District, Kenya, in the early 1990s led to the Al Fatah Peace Declaration (1993) and a set of local peacebuilding structures.
- A rapid response team (1994) and a permanent Peace and Development Committee (May 1995) brought elders, religious leaders, security forces, government and civil society together.
- They used traditional Somali dispute resolution (*xeer* and *diya*) alongside state structures.
- The Better Evidence Project rates the impact as "Lasting." Clashes still happen, and scaling the model beyond Wajir has been hard.
- It's an example of [[Peace Can Be Built Intentionally|peace built intentionally]] through ongoing local maintenance.

## Key facts (with page/section)
- **1994:** a rapid response team with local government and civil society members was set up. (Better Evidence Project, Conflict Relapse page)
- **1995:** civil society groups were united as a District Development Committee within local government. (same)
- **May 1995:** the WPDC was established as a permanent body with Kenyan government and international donor support. (same)
- **Members:** led by the District Commissioner; included military and security services, government departments, NGOs, elders, women, youth, religious leaders, the business community, and Wajir's four MPs. (same)
- **Council of Elders:** 10 clan leaders, formed to facilitate dialogue with the government. (same)
- **Methods:** traditional Somali *xeer* and *diya*; constituency boundaries adjusted so each clan had a parliamentary seat. (same)
- **Caveat:** a July 2000 uptick in violence caused 30 deaths; occasional cross-border clashes continue. (same)
- **29 September 1993:** Al Fatah Peace Declaration signed. (Better Evidence Project, Ending the Armed Conflict page, *(unverified)*: from a search summary)
- **Origins disputed:** Participedia credits Dekha Ibrahim's Wajir Women Association for Peace (1993); other accounts centre on the elders' council. *(unverified)*: from search summaries
- **Scaling:** Irenees notes that extending the model to other districts remains a challenge. *(unverified)*: from a search summary

> ## 1. Wajir, Kenya: early mediation and rapid response (1993 to present)
> **Method:** local peace committees and fast de-escalation, which is the "fund peacebuilding before conflicts start" idea.
>
> **Facts (sourced):**
> - Clan violence in Wajir District in the early 1990s led to the Al Fatah Peace Declaration. The Better Evidence Project dates it to 29 September 1993.
> - A **rapid response team** was set up in 1994. It brought together elders, religious leaders, security officers, local government and civil society, and went to all parties when a dispute flared.
> - It used **traditional Somali dispute resolution** (*xeer* and *diya*, customary law and compensation) rather than relying only on Kenyan courts.
> - In May 1995 the **Wajir Peace and Development Committee** became a permanent body, with Kenyan government and donor support.
> - **Outcome:** the Better Evidence Project rates the impact as **"Lasting."** In its words: "The measures taken in response to the violence of the early 1990s succeeded in reducing armed conflict in Wajir," and "Wajir remained one of the most peaceful districts in the entire country."
>
> **Caveats (sourced):** clashes still happen, including a July 2000 flare-up that killed 30 people. Irenees notes that spreading the model to other districts has been hard. Sources also disagree on who started it: Participedia credits Dekha Ibrahim's women's peace group (1993), while other accounts focus on the elders' council.
>
> **Analogy fit:** agriculture. Weeding never ends. The rapid response team is the permanent upkeep.
