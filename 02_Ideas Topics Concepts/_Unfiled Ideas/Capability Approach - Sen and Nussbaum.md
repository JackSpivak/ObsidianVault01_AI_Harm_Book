# Capability Approach - Sen and Nussbaum

## Why this is distinct
- The compendium mentions the Capabilities Approach only in a history note, as one entry among the flourishing-type harms. This note treats it as a full framework of its own: functionings, capabilities, agency, conversion factors, and what it rejects. Each of those parts could map onto the harm ontology.

## Anticipated direction
- Use it as an **evaluative standard** in the v3 model, where harm is judged from a perspective ([[HO-000 Harm as Perspective-Relative Assessment]]). In capability terms, harm means shrinking someone's capability set, not reducing their resources or their satisfaction.
- **Conversion factors** look like a ready-made vocabulary for patient-specific effect properties ([[HO-I.b Effect and Cascades]]): the same factor can affect two people differently.
- Its rejection of welfarism, because preferences **adapt to deprivation**, fits closely with [[HO-001 Harm by Habituation and Captured Assent]]. A person trained into deference may report being satisfied while their capabilities have shrunk.
- Sen left the list of capabilities open and Nussbaum fixed one. That echoes [[Open Harm Categories - Harm Beyond Human Experience]]: an open list leaves room for capabilities, and harms, that nobody has named yet.

## Conceptual territory
- Revisited. The Capabilities Approach already appears in [[Interdisciplinary_Harm_Ontology_Compendium v2a]] (historyNote: "Amartya Sen & Martha Nussbaum formalized the Capabilities Approach"). This note works it out as a perspective/standard rather than a sibling category.

## Summary
- Sen's capability approach judges well-being and justice by what people can actually do and be, not by their income, resources or reported happiness.
- **Functionings** are the beings and doings a person achieves. **Capabilities** are the real freedoms to achieve them. **Agency** is the ability to pursue the goals one values.
- **Conversion factors** (personal, social, environmental) decide whether a resource becomes a functioning. A bicycle gives no mobility to someone who can't ride it.
- It rejects utility measures, because people adapt their expectations to deprivation, and resource measures, because people convert resources differently.
- It shaped the UNDP's Human Development Index and redefined poverty as capability deprivation.
- Sen kept the list of capabilities open for democratic valuation. Nussbaum proposed a fixed list of ten central capabilities to be guaranteed constitutionally.

## Original wording
> "Amartya Sen's capability approach is a normative framework for evaluating human well-being, poverty, and social justice by focusing on what people are actually able to do and be, rather than merely what resources or income they possess."

> "• Functionings: The actual, achieved states of "beings and doings" that a person values, such as being well-nourished, healthy, educated, or participating in community life."
> "• Capabilities: The real, substantive opportunities and freedom ("choice sets") a person has to achieve those functionings. It emphasizes freedom of choice—having the option to live a valued life, not just stumbling into an outcome."
> "• Agency: A person's ability to act, pursue goals they value, and bring about change for themselves or society."
> "• Conversion Factors: Personal, social, and environmental conditions that affect how well a person can transform a commodity or resource into a functioning:
> 	• Personal: Age, metabolism, physical disability, or gender.
> 	• Social: Public policies, social norms, discrimination, or power structures.
> 	• Environmental: Climate, infrastructure, or geographic location.
> (For example, giving a bicycle to a person with a physical disability may not translate into the functioning of mobility due to a personal conversion factor)."

> "• Welfarism and Utility: Rejects measuring well-being purely by happiness, pleasure, or desire fulfillment, which can adapt to deprivation (e.g., chronically poor people lowering their expectations)."
> "• Resourcism and Opulence: Rejects judging a person's advantage strictly by GDP per capita, income, or primary goods (as in strict Rawlsian or utilitarian models), arguing that people have diverse abilities to convert those resources into actual living standards."

> "• Human Development: The framework fundamentally shaped the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and the creation of metrics like the Human Development Index (HDI), which shift focus from pure economic output to life expectancy, literacy, and living standards."
> "• Poverty Definition: Redefined poverty not merely as low income, but as capability deprivation—the denial of real opportunities to live a decent life."

> "While Amartya Sen deliberately left the list of important capabilities open-ended to allow for democratic and participatory local valuation, his frequent collaborator, philosopher Martha Nussbaum, developed a concrete list of ten central human capabilities (including life, health, bodily integrity, and practical reason) meant to be guaranteed by constitutional design."

## Why it matters to the book
- It gives a precise way to say how AI harms someone even when they're satisfied and well-resourced: their real options or their agency shrink.
- Adaptive preferences are the capability approach's warning that contented users aren't necessarily unharmed users.

## Open questions
- Should capability loss be one evaluative standard among several in v3, or the "measure of all of them" (as Aristotle argued in the Classical Council stress test)?
- Do AI systems act as a new kind of **conversion factor**, neither personal, social nor environmental?
- Sen's open list or Nussbaum's fixed list: which suits an ontology that is meant never to be complete?
