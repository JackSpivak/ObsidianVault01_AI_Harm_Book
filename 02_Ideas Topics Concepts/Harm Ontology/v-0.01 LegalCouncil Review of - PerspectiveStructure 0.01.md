## Why this is distinct
- The third of seven council reviews of [[PerspectiveStructure 0.01]]. It reads the structure through legal theory (Hart, Dworkin, Holmes, Scalia, Ginsburg, Fuller).

## Anticipated direction
- Its proposals (an **Institutional** group, the description/evaluation split, date-stamped meanings, procedural integrity) feed into PerspectiveStructure 0.02.

## Conceptual territory
- New. Applies jurisprudential vocabulary to the attribute structure. Builds on [[HO-II.c Judgment as Assessment - Reasoning over Precedent|HO-II.c]].

## Summary
- **Gaps:** recognition status, validity vs efficacy, internal acceptance, open texture, principles, interpretive concepts, enforcement reality, incentive effects, canonical text, meaning-as-of dates, limitation periods and discovery rules, differential burden, remedy, change mode, procedural integrity, jurisdiction, standing, standard of proof, precedential weight, causation test, legal personhood.
- **Structure:** separate description from evaluation (Hart); version everything by date (Scalia); add a books-vs-action layer (Holmes); allow both principles and text (Dworkin vs Scalia). Proposed new group: **Institutional**.
- **Meaning:** `authority` splits into validity, efficacy and acceptance; interpretive concepts need Divergence links, not altLabels; `temporalScope` needs limitation periods; `moralCircle` has a legal twin in `legalPersonhood`; `criterionOfTruth` becomes standard of proof and causation test; `whoseVoice` becomes standing.
- **Convergence:** stated vs operative congruence now has three sources (Confucius, Kant, Fuller).
- **Most book-relevant:** *Loomis* (secret rules), *Ledbetter* (harms that time out before discovery), differential enforcement.

---

**Hart.** Start with a distinction your structure blurs: what a lens *is* versus whether it is *good*. `blindSpot: negligent`, `misnaming` and `failureByExcess` are evaluations, filed alongside descriptions like `lineage`. Keep them apart. Evaluations are themselves Assessments made from a perspective, so they need an `assessedBy`.

Second, `authority` is ambiguous. A lens can be *legally valid*, meaning a system's rule of recognition makes its verdicts binding, without being *effective*, and the reverse is also true. Record `recognitionStatus` (which legal systems recognize this lens as a source, and at what rank) separately from `authority` in fact. Finally, every lens has a core of settled cases and a penumbra of open texture: "no vehicles in the park". Record where the penumbra lies.

**Dworkin.** Hart's split is tidy, but it misses how lenses actually decide hard cases: by *principles*, not rules. In *Riggs v. Palmer* (1889) the court denied a murderer his inheritance under a valid will because "no one shall profit from his own wrong". Your structure has `assumption` but no `principles`. Those are the weighted, non-mechanical commitments a lens falls back on when its rules run out. Also, "harm" and "fairness" are *interpretive concepts*. People don't merely define them differently by domain; they *disagree* about them. `definitionByDomain` makes that disagreement look like translation. Flag interpretive concepts so the disagreement shows up as a Divergence, not as a list of synonyms.

**Holmes.** Gentlemen, the life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience. Look at any lens the way the bad man looks at law: what will actually happen to me? Your structure records what a lens claims. It doesn't record what it *does*. Add `enforcementReality`: how often its verdicts are actually applied, by whom, and with what discretion. Pound called this the gap between law in books and law in action. A content policy that is enforced against one community and not another is two lenses wearing one name.

**Scalia.** My colleagues want principles and predictions. I want the *text*. Which words, adopted when, constitute this lens? Your provenance group records sources but not the **authoritative formulation**, and meanings drift. "Harm" in Mill's 1859 sense isn't "harm" in a 2025 trust-and-safety policy. Add `canonicalText` and `meaningAsOf`, a date-stamp on every definition. Otherwise your ontology lets later readers smuggle their preferences into older words, and calls it evolution.

**Ginsburg.** Justice Scalia and I disagreed often, and we agree that dates matter. But I'd point to the dates that shut people out. In *Ledbetter v. Goodyear* (2007) the Court held that a 180-day filing period ran from the first discriminatory pay decision, before the plaintiff could have known about it. I dissented, and Congress reversed the result in 2009. `temporalScope` needs `limitationPeriod` and `discoveryRule`, because a harm that can't be seen can't be challenged in time (HO-I.c).

Next, `whoseInterests` and `costBearer` must ask whether facially neutral rules fall unequally: `differentialBurden`, or disparate impact. Then `remedy`. A lens that names a harm but offers no remedy is commentary. And favour durable, incremental change. Lenses that leap too far invite backlash.

**Fuller.** Let me audit the lens as a set of rules. In *The Morality of Law* I named eight ways to fail to make law at all: no general rules, rules not published, retroactive rules, unclear rules, contradictory rules, rules impossible to obey, rules constantly changing, and **official action that doesn't match the declared rule**. Apply that to AI. In *State v. Loomis* (2016) a man was sentenced partly by COMPAS, a proprietary risk score whose workings he couldn't examine. That's an unpublished rule. Add `proceduralIntegrity`, scored against the eight desiderata. The eighth, congruence, is the formal version of Confucius's misnaming and Kant's hidden maxim. Three councils have now reached the same point.

**Proposed new group:** **Institutional** (validity, efficacy, recognition, acceptance, enforcement, standing, remedy, precedential weight and procedural integrity).

### 2. Divergence from the structure itself

- **Hart:** separate description from evaluation. The evaluative attributes (`blindSpot` type, `misnaming`, failures) should be Assessments with `assessedBy`, not plain facts about the lens. This is the strongest structural point so far, and it applies the ontology's own premise ([[HO-premise No Intrinsic Harmfulness|HO-premise]]) to lenses themselves.
- **Scalia:** the structure is atemporal. Every definition and text needs a date, or `PerspectiveType` will silently drift. This suggests versioning (`validFrom` / `validTo`) across the whole structure.
- **Holmes:** add a books-vs-action layer. Every claimed attribute may have an *observed* counterpart.
- **Dworkin vs Scalia:** principles vs text. The structure should allow both (`principles` and `canonicalText`) and record which one a lens relies on when they conflict.

### 3. Divergence on the meaning of existing attributes

- **`authority`** splits three ways: validity, efficacy and acceptance (Hart and Holmes), plus the legitimacy conditions from the Ethics review.
- **`definitionByDomain`** treats disagreement as translation. Interpretive concepts need a Divergence link, not altLabels (Dworkin).
- **`temporalScope`** needs a legal edge: limitation periods and discovery rules decide whether a harm can be heard at all (Ginsburg).
- **`moralCircle`** has a legal twin, `legalPersonhood`, and the two often diverge. Corporations are legal persons; animals generally aren't. The Whanganui River has been a legal person since 2017.
- **`criterionOfTruth`** becomes concrete in law as `standardOfProof` and `causationTest`.
- **`whoseVoice`** becomes concrete as `standing`: the formal gate on who may speak (*TransUnion*, HO-002 T08).

- **Main tension:** Hart and Scalia want clear sources and texts. Dworkin wants principles. Holmes says only enforcement matters. Fuller says form itself carries morality. Ginsburg asks who the neutral form leaves behind.
- **Strongest additions:** an **Institutional** group; the **description/evaluation split**; **date-stamped meanings**; Fuller's **procedural integrity** as an audit of AI rules.

Crenshaw's intersectionality adds that burdens can fall on combinations of groups invisible when each is checked alone. **Limit:** choosing comparison groups is itself a perspectival choice. |

Madison*, 5 U.S. 137 (1803) | The maxim "where there is a right there is a remedy" was stated by Blackstone and quoted in *Marbury* (1803). Modern law distinguishes compensatory, injunctive, declaratory and restorative remedies; restorative justice developed from the 1970s. | William Blackstone; John Marshall; Howard Zehr (restorative justice) | A lens that names a harm but offers no remedy is commentary, as Ginsburg says, and many AI-ethics frameworks are exactly that. Recording `remedy` as an object property separates lenses that diagnose from lenses that repair. It also exposes mismatches: money damages poorly fit harms like reputational damage from a hallucinated defamatory claim, or loss of a chance at a job. Some AI harms need new remedy types, such as model retraining, data deletion including from trained weights, or algorithmic disgorgement (ordered by the US FTC in some cases, details unverified). For the book, the gap between harms that can be named and harms that can be remedied is a strong theme. |
| changeMode | incrementalism; judicial minimalism; backlash | Whether a lens develops by small, durable steps or sweeping change | Ginsburg's view that *Roe v. Wade* (1973) moved too far too fast and invited backlash | Ginsburg, "Speaking in a Judicial Voice", *NYU Law Review* 67 (1992): 1185; Sunstein, *One Case at a Time* (Harvard UP, 1999) | Ginsburg's 1992 Madison Lecture argued for measured motions. Cass Sunstein (1999) theorized judicial minimalism. The backlash debate intensified after *Dobbs* (2022) overruled *Roe*. | Ruth Bader Ginsburg; Cass Sunstein; Alexander Bickel ("passive virtues") | How a lens changes is part of what it is. Incremental lenses (common law, minimalist courts) adapt case by case and accumulate legitimacy slowly; sweeping lenses can transform a field quickly but provoke counter-movements. For AI governance this is a live strategic question: comprehensive regimes like the EU AI Act versus sectoral, case-by-case approaches. Recording `changeMode` alongside history helps explain why some lenses are stable and others oscillate. It also links to the Classical review's `failureByExcess`/`failureByDeficiency`: too incremental, and harms outpace the law; too sweeping, and rules lose acceptance. This is partly descriptive and partly evaluative, so per Hart's split the evaluative judgment ("too fast") should be an Assessment, not a fact. |
| proceduralIntegrity | inner morality of law; rule of law; procedural natural law | Whether a lens's rules satisfy Fuller's eight desiderata: generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy, and congruence between declared rule and official action | *State v. Loomis*: sentencing informed by a proprietary risk score the defendant couldn't examine | Fuller, *The Morality of Law* (Yale UP, 1964; rev. 1969); *State v. Loomis*, 881 N.W.2d 749 (Wis. 2016) | Fuller set out the eight desiderata through the parable of King Rex, who fails at lawmaking in eight ways. The Hart–Fuller debate (1958) over Nazi law and the "grudge informer" cases preceded it. | Lon L. Fuller; H. L. A. Hart (critic); Joseph Raz ("The Rule of Law and its Virtue", 1977) | Fuller's desiderata are a ready-made audit for algorithmic rules, which often fail several at once: unpublished (secret models), unclear (opaque features), constantly changing (retraining), impossible to comply with (unknown criteria), and incongruent (stated purpose vs actual effect). Scoring each desideratum as an object gives the ontology a structured way to say *how* a lens fails procedurally, not just that it does. The eighth desideratum, congruence, is the formal version of Confucius's misnaming and Kant's operative maxim, so three councils converge on it. Hart's objection is worth recording: the desiderata are conditions of efficacy, compatible with great iniquity, so procedural integrity is not moral goodness. The ontology should keep them as separate attributes. |
| jurisdiction | jurisdiction; territorial and subject-matter scope; extraterritoriality | The territory, persons and subject matter over which a lens's verdicts apply | GDPR applies to non-EU firms offering services to people in the EU | GDPR Art. 3 (2016); *Microsoft Corp. v. United States* (2d Cir. 2016) (data stored abroad) | Territorial jurisdiction dates from the Westphalian state system (1648). The internet strained it from the 1990s (Johnson & Post, "Law and Borders", *Stanford Law Review* 48, 1996). The GDPR's extraterritorial reach (the "Brussels effect", Bradford 2012/2020) extended EU norms globally. | David Johnson and David Post; Anu Bradford | Jurisdiction refines `domainOfApplicability` into something legally precise: which territory, which persons, which subject matter. It also explains a central feature of AI harm: models built in one jurisdiction affect people in many others, often with no forum available to them. The Brussels effect shows one lens spreading by market power rather than legal authority, which is worth recording as `efficacy` beyond `validity`. For modelling, jurisdiction should be a set of object links (territory, persons, subject matter), because the three can diverge. A US company, an EU user and a Kenyan data worker can sit in three regimes at once. **Limit:** jurisdiction tells you where a lens may apply, not whether anyone will apply it (see `enforcementReality`). |
| standing | standing; *locus standi*; injury in fact | Who is entitled to invoke a lens before a forum | *TransUnion v. Its requirement of concrete, particular injury systematically excludes diffuse, probabilistic and latent harms, which are exactly the harms typical of AI: risk of misuse, aggregated privacy loss, exposure to biased scoring that hasn't yet produced a decision. *TransUnion* is already test case T08 in [[HO-002 Ontology Stress-Test Suite\|HO-002]]. Other systems handle standing differently: EU law allows representative actions by consumer and data-protection organizations, which partly bridges the gap. Recording standing criteria per forum makes visible a powerful blind spot: harms the law recognizes in principle but no one can bring. |
| standardOfProof | burden and standard of proof; preponderance; beyond reasonable doubt | How certain a forum must be before it accepts a claim, and who must prove it | Civil: more likely than not; criminal: beyond reasonable doubt | *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); *Addington v. The EU's proposed AI Liability Directive (withdrawn in 2025, unverified) would have eased the burden on claimants through presumptions of causality. The precautionary principle shows an alternative where the burden falls on the party creating the risk. Recording both the standard and who bears the burden lets the ontology compare lenses on how much uncertainty they tolerate and on whose side they let it fall. |
| precedentialWeight | stare decisis; binding vs persuasive authority; overruling | How far a lens's prior verdicts bind later ones | *Dobbs* (2022) overruling *Roe* (1973) and *Casey* (1992) | *Planned Parenthood v. Civil-law systems give precedent less formal weight. *Casey* (1992) set factors for overruling; *Dobbs* (2022) applied a different analysis. | William Blackstone; Benjamin Cardozo (*The Nature of the Judicial Process*, 1921); Frederick Schauer | Precedential weight decides whether a lens accumulates and stabilizes its own judgments or starts fresh each time. That's central to [[HO-II.c Judgment as Assessment - Reasoning over Precedent\|HO-II.c]], which already models judgments as Assessments that can reason over precedent. Recording weight as binding, persuasive or none per forum lets the ontology trace how one Assessment constrains later ones. For AI, the question is pressing: early AI-harm decisions will set patterns that are hard to reverse. Automated decision systems also create *de facto* precedent: a model trained on past decisions encodes them as binding, without any overruling mechanism. That's an inversion worth naming for the book. **Limit:** precedent's authority depends on institutional acceptance, so it can collapse quickly, as *Dobbs* shows. |
| causationTest | but-for causation; proximate cause; material contribution | The test a lens uses to decide whether a factor caused a harm | *Palsgraf*: a railroad wasn't liable for injury to a distant bystander from an unforeseeable explosion | *Palsgraf v. Hart and Honoré (1959) analysed common-sense causation in law. Material-contribution tests developed for multi-cause harms like industrial disease. | Benjamin Cardozo; William Andrews; H. L. A. Hart and Tony Honoré | Causation tests decide which factors count as causes, which is exactly the question [[HO-I.a Factor and CausalRole\|HO-I.a]], [[HO-003 Causal Assembly - the Perfect Storm\|HO-003]] and [[HO-005 Assessed Membership and Systematic Omission\|HO-005]] address. Law shows that the answer is a policy choice, not a fact: proximate cause is explicitly a judgment about where liability should stop. For AI harm, distributed causation is the norm: data suppliers, model developers, deployers and users all contribute, and no single one may pass a but-for test. That creates a "many hands" problem in which harm occurs without an identifiable cause. Recording which causation test a lens uses, and its foreseeability limits, makes visible why some AI harms fall through every lens. |

Modelling the reasonable-person standard as a Perspective attached to a lens lets the ontology ask whose standpoint it actually represents. **Limit:** the standard is meant to be idealized, so any description of it is contestable. |

## Real-world examples
- *State v. Loomis* (2016): sentencing informed by a proprietary risk score (unpublished rule).
- *Ledbetter v. Goodyear* (2007): a harm that timed out before it could be discovered.
- Differential enforcement of platform policies: one name, two lenses.

## Why it matters to the book
- Law is the lens with power to bind. Its gates (standing, limitation, causation, proof) decide which AI harms can be heard at all, and many AI harms fall through them.
