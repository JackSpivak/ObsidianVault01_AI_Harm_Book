Amartya Sen’s capability approach is a normative framework for evaluating human well-being, poverty, and social justice by focusing on what people are actually able to do and be, rather than merely what resources or income they possess. [1] (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/), [2] (https://commonweb.unifr.ch/artsdean/pub/gestens/f/as/files/4760/24995_105422.pdf), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WWEGvW0Irq0)

Core Concepts

• Functionings: The actual, achieved states of "beings and doings" that a person values, such as being well-nourished, healthy, educated, or participating in community life. [1] (https://iep.utm.edu/sen-cap/), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capability_approach), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sab2nq1wdr0)
• Capabilities: The real, substantive opportunities and freedom ("choice sets") a person has to achieve those functionings. It emphasizes freedom of choice—having the option to live a valued life, not just stumbling into an outcome. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capability_approach), [2] (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sab2nq1wdr0)
• Agency: A person's ability to act, pursue goals they value, and bring about change for themselves or society. [1] (https://www.scribd.com/document/836888692/Capability-approach-Amartya-Sen), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capability_approach), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sab2nq1wdr0)
• Conversion Factors: Personal, social, and environmental conditions that affect how well a person can transform a commodity or resource into a functioning:
	• Personal: Age, metabolism, physical disability, or gender.
	• Social: Public policies, social norms, discrimination, or power structures.
	• Environmental: Climate, infrastructure, or geographic location.
(For example, giving a bicycle to a person with a physical disability may not translate into the functioning of mobility due to a personal conversion factor). [1] (https://learn.mru.org/lessons/amartyasen-1-capabilities), [2] (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sab2nq1wdr0)

What It Rejects

Sen’s approach explicitly moves away from traditional economic and philosophical metrics:
• Welfarism and Utility: Rejects measuring well-being purely by happiness, pleasure, or desire fulfillment, which can adapt to deprivation (e.g., chronically poor people lowering their expectations). [1] (https://iep.utm.edu/sen-cap/), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sab2nq1wdr0)
• Resourcism and Opulence: Rejects judging a person's advantage strictly by GDP per capita, income, or primary goods (as in strict Rawlsian or utilitarian models), arguing that people have diverse abilities to convert those resources into actual living standards. [1] (https://base.socioeco.org/docs/developments_critiques_advances.pdf), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fhzD-yCJvfM&t=125), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sab2nq1wdr0)

Real-World Impact

• Human Development: The framework fundamentally shaped the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and the creation of metrics like the Human Development Index (HDI), which shift focus from pure economic output to life expectancy, literacy, and living standards. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WWEGvW0Irq0), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capability_approach), [3] (https://learn.mru.org/lessons/amartyasen-1-capabilities)
• Poverty Definition: Redefined poverty not merely as low income, but as capability deprivation—the denial of real opportunities to live a decent life. [1] (https://commonweb.unifr.ch/artsdean/pub/gestens/f/as/files/4760/24995_105422.pdf), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WWEGvW0Irq0)

Sen vs. Nussbaum

While Amartya Sen deliberately left the list of important capabilities open-ended to allow for democratic and participatory local valuation, his frequent collaborator, philosopher Martha Nussbaum, developed a concrete list of ten central human capabilities (including life, health, bodily integrity, and practical reason) meant to be guaranteed by constitutional design. [1] (https://iep.utm.edu/sen-cap/), [2] (https://commonweb.unifr.ch/artsdean/pub/gestens/f/as/files/4760/24995_105422.pdf), [3] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capability_approach), [4] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WWEGvW0Irq0)

Beyond the core structural benefits already discussed (eliminating drift, standardizing terminology, enforcing completeness, deterministic inference, auditability, reproducibility, decoupled governance, knowledge-graph querying, and mitigating training-data popularity bias), removing brevity constraints reveals **14 additional technical, epistemological, legal, and operational advantages** of using a formal ontology rather than relying solely on a trained LLM for harm analysis:

---

### I. Epistemological & Analytical Advantages

1. **Explicit Preservation of Normative Boundaries (`skos:scopeNote` Exclusion Rules):**
An LLM relies on statistical association, often blurring neighboring concepts together. A formal ontology explicitly encodes negative boundaries—defining not just what a harm *is*, but what it *is not* (e.g., strictly separating actionable `harm:InjuriaSineDamnoHarm` from non-actionable competitive losses like *Damnum Absque Injuria*, or distinguishing `harm:ProbabilisticRiskHarm` from actualized injury).


2. **Protection Against "Fluency Bias" & Persuasive Hallucination:**
LLMs are optimized to produce coherent, confident, and persuasive prose. When analyzing ambiguous scenarios, an LLM can invent plausible-sounding moral or legal rationalizations that mask analytical gaps. An ontology forces claims into rigid, falsifiable slots—if a required property (such as `harm:hasCausationMechanism` or `harm:hasVictim`) cannot be grounded in evidence, the gap remains visibly empty rather than papered over with eloquent prose.


3. **Preservation of Cross-Disciplinary Tension Without Premature Synthesis:**
In complex socio-technical scenarios, philosophical ethics, tort law, behavioral economics, and AI safety often disagree on whether an event constitutes a harm. An LLM naturally smooths over these tensions to produce a unified summary. An ontology allows multiple conflicting frameworks (e.g., `harm:HedonicHarm` vs. `harm:PerfectionistTeleologicalHarm` vs. `harm:KantianDeontologicalHarm`) to coexist explicitly on the same event record, preserving theoretical pluralism.


4. **Separation of Descriptive Fact from Normative Judgment:**
LLMs frequently conflate empirical setbacks with moral wrongness due to alignment tuning (RLHF). An ontology structurally decouples the empirical mechanics of an event (`harm:hasComparativeBaseline`, `harm:hasCausationMechanism`, `harm:hasSeverityMagnitude`) from its moral or legal status (`harm:hasNormativeStatus`: `harm:NonNormativeSetback` vs. `harm:NormativeWrongful`), allowing analysts to record factual damage without prematurely assigning blame.



---

### II. Computational & Engineering Advantages

5. **Token-Window & Context-Budget Efficiency:**
Prompting an LLM with tens of thousands of words of legal doctrine, philosophical definitions, and edge-case rules on every API call is expensive, slow, and degrades attention over long contexts ("lost in the middle"). An ontology compresses domain expertise into lightweight, high-density URIs and class hierarchies that can be injected selectively or traversed externally via graph queries.
6. **Composable & Modular Architecture (`owl:imports`):**
Formal ontologies are modular by design. A general harm ontology can cleanly import domain-specific sub-ontologies (e.g., medical bioethics, financial compliance, cybersecurity CVEs, or child safety) without breaking existing definitions or requiring a monolithic model update.
7. **Automated Completeness & Missing-Data Detection (SHACL / ShEx Validation):**
Using graph validation languages like SHACL (Shapes Constraint Language), an ontology-driven pipeline can mathematically verify whether a harm assessment is structurally valid before it is accepted—for example, automatically rejecting an assessment of `harm:PrivateTortiousHarm` if it lacks a populated `harm:hasPerpetrator`, `harm:hasVictim`, and `harm:hasRemedialDutyBearer`.


8. **Backward & Forward Schema Migration:**
When a harm taxonomy evolves (e.g., splitting a broad class into two finer-grained subclasses), formal ontology versioning (`owl:equivalentClass`, `owl:deprecated`, `skos:broader`/`skos:narrower`) allows historical harm logs to be migrated and re-queried programmatically. With raw LLM text outputs, historical assessments must be completely re-run from scratch whenever analytical definitions change.
9. **Lightweight Edge & Air-Gapped Execution:**
Once events are mapped into ontological triples, downstream rule evaluation, risk triage, and compliance checks can run in milliseconds on standard CPUs without GPU infrastructure, external API calls, or internet connectivity—critical for classified, air-gapped, or high-throughput real-time systems.

---

### III. Governance, Legal & Institutional Advantages

10. **Decoupling Domain Policy from Proprietary AI Safety Filters:**
Commercial LLMs embed opaque, vendor-controlled safety guardrails that may refuse to analyze sensitive harms (e.g., self-harm, violence, hate speech, or biological risks) or apply a single corporation's moral stance. An external ontology allows an organization, court, or research institution to define and enforce *its own* transparent, domain-appropriate harm criteria independently of the underlying foundation model.
11. **Multi-Stakeholder & Democratic Deliberation:**
Non-technical stakeholders—philosophers, judges, economists, ethicists, regulators, and affected communities—can directly read, debate, and amend a structured ontology (as demonstrated by the Joint Councils structure in your compendium). By contrast, modifying an LLM's internal behavior requires specialized machine learning engineering (fine-tuning, RLHF, or activation steering).


12. **Standardized Cross-Institutional Data Sharing & Federated Reporting:**
When hospitals, regulators, AI labs, and insurers need to pool incident data (e.g., tracking systemic AI failures or public health hazards), free-text LLM reports create insurmountable entity-resolution problems. Shared ontological URIs allow disparate institutions to aggregate harm telemetry without exposing sensitive raw text.
13. **Counterfactual & Sensitivity Simulation:**
Because ontological attributes are discrete, structured variables, analysts can run deterministic counterfactual simulations—flipping a single property (e.g., changing `harm:hasModality` from `harm:ProbabilisticRisk` to `harm:ActualizedHarm`, or `harm:hasScalabilityAsymmetry` from `harm:HighFrictionProportional` to `harm:ZeroMarginalCostReplication`) to observe how downstream legal standing, duty-bearing, and risk scores shift.


14. **Institutional Memory & Pedagogical Scaffolding:**
An ontology captures not just labels, but intellectual lineage (`skos:historyNote`, `dcterms:bibliographicCitation`). It serves as an enduring, self-documenting institutional knowledge base that trains both human analysts and AI agents in the precise conceptual heritage behind every harm category, preventing institutional amnesia as teams and models turn over.

**ai**: **Short answer:** yes, there are respected, published, open ontologies in current use. Most of them build on AIRO and come from one research community. None that I found models harm the way your ontology does. AIRO models *risk management* (in the ISO 31000 sense: risk source → consequence → impact), not what harm *is* or how it gets *judged*. That's where your work adds something.

### 1. What exists and is available

| Resource | Status / standing | Machine-readable? | What it covers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AIRO** (AI Risk Ontology), ADAPT Centre: Golpayegani, Pandit, Lewis, 2022 | Peer-reviewed; open source; v1.0 | **Yes**: OWL/Turtle at w3id.org/airo; repo `DelaramGlp/airo`; a parsed `.ttl` mirror exists | Risk, RiskSource, Consequence, Impact (subclass of Consequence), ImpactArea, AISubject, built on the EU AI Act and ISO 31000/31073 |
| **VAIR** (Vocabulary of AI Risks) | Draft, "no official standing" | Yes (w3id.org/vair) | The taxonomy of specific terms that fills AIRO |
| **W3C DPV**: RISK and AI extensions (v2.3, February 2026) | W3C *Community Group* report, **not** a W3C Standard | Yes | A taxonomy of consequences and impacts including "harm", financial loss, data breach, and also *positive* consequences (benefits). **AIRO and VAIR have been incorporated into DPV.** Models the AI Act's risk definition. |
| **AI Incident Database**: CSET AI Harm Taxonomy, GMF taxonomy | Widely used for classifying incidents | Database snapshot download; formats not confirmed | Harm types and severity (CSET); goals/methods/failures (GMF) |
| **MIT AI Risk Repository** | Widely cited; living database | Spreadsheet/database (not OWL) | 1,700+ risks; 7 domains / 24 subdomains; causal taxonomy |
| **LKIF-Core** (legal ontology, ESTRELLA project) | Established in AI & Law research | OWL | Basic legal concepts: agents, actions, rights, roles. **Tort and harm appear only as a planned extension.** |
| **IBM Risk Atlas Nexus** | Open source (per earlier search) | Not confirmed this round | Combines several frameworks; reuses AIRO |

### 2. Does your ontology add anything to AIRO?
This comparison is based on search summaries and AIRO's class list. **I haven't run a full comparison of the files yet.** With that caveat, these are likely additions, judging from your v1/v2a and v3 notes:

1. **Theories of what harm is.** AIRO treats Impact as an effect on people or the environment. It doesn't distinguish hedonic, interest-based, rights-based, flourishing (capabilities) or Kantian accounts of harm. Your compendium does.
2. **Legal doctrine inside the model.** Doctrines like *injuria sine damno* and *damnum absque injuria*, the line between tort and crime, and remedial duty-bearers. Even LKIF-Core leaves tort as future work.
3. **Fact separated from verdict.** AIRO assumes a consequence is negative. Your v3 says nothing is harmful in itself: judgments are made from perspectives, with time horizons, and disagreement between judgments is modelled as part of the record (HO-000, HO-II, HO-III.a). I found nothing like that in AIRO, VAIR or DPV.
4. **Harm mechanisms specific to AI:** habituation and captured assent (HO-001), assemblies of individually harmless factors (HO-003), how widely a harm can spread at almost no extra cost, and observability and experienceability (HO-I.c).
5. **Long-form narrative and lineage:** history notes and citations across disciplines. AIRO's annotations are short and standards-based.

**Where AIRO and DPV are stronger:** regulatory alignment (EU AI Act, ISO), institutional uptake through DPV, AI-system modelling (purpose, capability, deployer, controls and mitigations), and published vocabularies with stable identifiers.
